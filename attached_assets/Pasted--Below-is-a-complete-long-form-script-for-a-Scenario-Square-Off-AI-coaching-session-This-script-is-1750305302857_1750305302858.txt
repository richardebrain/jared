 Below is a complete, long-form script for a Scenario Square-Off AI coaching session. This script is written as if the AI mentor is interacting with a preschool teacher who is struggling with a difficult child. It follows the full arc of a meaningful coaching conversation: opening prompt → emotional validation → deeper inquiry → reframing → practical strategy → encouragement.

🗣️ Scenario Square-Off: Full Coaching Script
AI Mentor:
Hey there—let’s take a breath and check in. What’s something in your classroom that’s been really challenging for you lately?

Teacher:
Honestly? There’s a child in my class who hits me. He hits the other kids too. I’ve tried everything, and nothing works. I think he hates me.

AI Mentor:
Wow. That must be really tough—and honestly, painful too. Getting hit—especially when you’re showing up every day to care for children—can feel incredibly discouraging. I want to acknowledge that before we try to fix anything. You deserve to feel safe and respected in your classroom.

Let’s talk about it. Can you tell me more about when the hitting usually happens? Is it during certain times of day or activities?

Teacher:
It’s mostly during transitions—like when we have to clean up or go outside. He’ll scream, throw toys, and hit me if I get too close.

AI Mentor:
Thank you. That’s helpful context. Transitions can be a major trigger, especially for kids who struggle with change or control. You mentioned he screams and throws too—so it’s more than just hitting—it’s a whole-body reaction.

Why do you think transitions are so hard for him? Any patterns you’ve noticed?

Teacher:
I don’t know… maybe he just doesn’t like being told what to do. Or maybe he’s spoiled at home.

AI Mentor:
It’s normal to go there—we all make quick assumptions when we’re under stress. But I wonder… what if it’s not about being spoiled? What if it’s about not feeling safe or in control during transitions?

Kids who act out during changes are often kids whose brains are firing off alarms. Do you know if he has a consistent home routine or anything that might be causing extra stress right now?

Teacher:
I’m not sure. I think mom works late and grandma watches him sometimes.

AI Mentor:
That’s good information. Inconsistent caregiving can definitely impact a child’s emotional regulation. It doesn’t make the behavior okay—but it can help us understand it.

Here’s something to consider: what if, instead of seeing his hitting as defiance, we saw it as a stress signal? His brain may be saying, “I don’t know what’s coming next—I feel out of control—this is how I survive.”

Does that shift anything for you?

Teacher:
Yeah… I guess it does. I never thought about it like that. It doesn’t excuse it, but it explains it.

AI Mentor:
Exactly. Behavior is communication. And when we decode it, we can intervene in a way that builds connection and safety instead of just managing symptoms.

So here’s a practical strategy that’s worked in other classrooms:
✅ Give him a “transition buddy” job—something small he can control.
✅ Use a visual timer or picture schedule so he can see what’s next.
✅ Give a 15-second warning with a calm tone before transitions. Even just: “In a few seconds, it’s clean-up time. Let’s get ready.”

Would you be willing to try one of those and see how he responds?

Teacher:
I could try giving him a cleanup job. He likes to hold the broom for some reason.

AI Mentor:
That’s perfect. That broom might be his “security object”—a familiar thing that grounds him.

Remember: your goal isn’t perfection—it’s progress. The more safety and predictability you build, the less his body will feel the need to lash out.

And one more thing: you’re doing better than you think. The fact that you’re here, thinking about this, talking it out—that already makes you the kind of teacher who changes lives.

Teacher:
Thanks. I really needed that.

AI Mentor:
Anytime. Let’s check back in after a few days of trying this and see what shifts. You’ve got this.
